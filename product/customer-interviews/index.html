Discovery

Five questions I ask in every customer interview

"Would you use this feature?" is the most common interview question and the least useful one. People are poor predictors of their own future. They are excellent witnesses of their own past.

DataKraft · 4 min read

Almost every customer says yes when asked whether they would like a new feature. It costs them nothing, and they want to be helpful. That is why I keep interviews about what people actually did, not what they might do. These five questions cover most of what I need.

1. "Tell me about the last time you…"

Start with a specific, recent event: the last time they exported a report, onboarded a colleague or tried to pay an invoice. Specific memories are detailed and honest. General opinions are neither.

2. "What were you trying to get done?"

This uncovers the goal behind the task. Someone exporting a report may really be preparing for a meeting with their boss. That changes what a good solution looks like.

3. "What was the hardest part?"

Let them describe the friction in their own words. Then stay quiet. The most useful detail usually comes after a pause, when the person adds "and actually, what really annoyed me was…".

4. "What did you do about it?"

This is the question that separates real problems from mild annoyances. If they built a spreadsheet, asked a colleague or paid for another tool, the problem matters. If they did nothing, it probably does not, however loudly they complain.

The workaround is the evidence. A problem nobody works around is rarely worth solving first.

5. "Who else should I talk to?"

End every interview by asking for the next one. It keeps discovery going without extra recruiting effort, and it often leads to the person who actually feels the problem most.

After the interview

  • Write down quotes, not interpretations, within the hour.
  • Note every workaround: it is the strongest signal you will get.
  • After five to eight interviews, look for problems that came up independently in at least three.

Those repeated problems are the input for the next step: turning feedback into a ranked backlog.
