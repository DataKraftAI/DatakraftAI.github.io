Discovery

From 40 customer complaints to a ranked backlog in one afternoon

Most teams are not short of feedback. They are short of a way to turn it into a decision. Here is the method I use, step by step.

DataKraft · 6 min read

Every product team has the same pile: support tickets, sales notes, app store reviews, comments from the last customer call. It all feels urgent, and it all comes from someone who matters. The usual result is a backlog sorted by whoever asked last or loudest.

The method below takes about three hours for 40 pieces of feedback. It produces a short ranked list that you can show to stakeholders and defend line by line.

1. Collect into one place, word for word

Copy every complaint into one sheet, one row each, in the customer's own words. Add three columns: source (ticket, call, review), customer type (new, long-time, paying plan) and date.

Do not summarise yet. "Can't find the export button" and "export is broken" may sound like the same thing, but they lead to very different fixes.

2. Cluster by the problem, not by the feature

Read through the rows and give each one a short problem label, written from the customer's side: "can't get my data out", not "CSV export". Aim for six to ten labels. If a row fits two labels, choose the one closer to the root cause.

Feature labels lock you into the first solution someone suggested. Problem labels keep the door open for a better one.

3. Count, then weigh

Count the rows per label. Then add one more column, because ten complaints from trial users are not the same as three from your largest customers. I use a simple weight: 1 for free users, 2 for paying users, 3 for at-risk or high-value accounts.

The table is an example, but the pattern is typical: the loudest topic in reviews is rarely the one with the most weight behind it.

4. Frame the top three as problem statements

For each of the top three labels, write one sentence: who has the problem, what they are trying to do, and how we will know it is solved.

Example: New users who connect their bank account during sign-up often give up when the connection fails. We will know this is solved when more new users reach their first transaction within a day.

If you cannot name the metric, the problem is not ready to be worked on yet. That is useful information too.

5. Score the options with RICE

Now, and only now, list possible solutions for each problem and score them: Reach (how many users per quarter), Impact (0.25 to 3), Confidence (how sure you are, in %) and Effort (person-weeks). The score is Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort.

The number itself matters less than the conversation it creates. When a stakeholder disagrees with the ranking, you can ask which of the four inputs they would change, and why. That is a far better discussion than "my feature is more important than yours".

What you end up with

  • One sheet with every complaint, traceable back to its source.
  • Six to ten problem labels with weighted counts.
  • Three framed problem statements, each with a success metric.
  • A ranked list of solutions with transparent scores.

It is not a perfect process. But it is visible, repeatable and quick, and it replaces "who shouted last" with something the whole team can argue with constructively.
