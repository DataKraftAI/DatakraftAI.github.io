Reporting

The one-page status report stakeholders actually read

Most status reports are green until the week they turn red. The problem is rarely dishonesty. It is the lack of clear rules for what each colour means.

DataKraft · 5 min read

Senior stakeholders give a status report about two minutes. In that time they want to know three things: are we on track, what could go wrong, and do you need anything from me? A good report answers exactly those questions, on one page, in the same format every week.

1. Define the colours before you need them

Agree with the sponsor at the start of the project what red, amber and green mean. Write it down. Then the colour is a fact, not a mood.

StatusRule (example)
GreenThe 85% forecast date is on or before the committed date, and no risk is rated high
AmberThe 85% date is up to two weeks late, or a high risk has no mitigation yet
RedThe 85% date is more than two weeks late, or a decision is overdue that blocks the team

With rules like these, a project can not stay "green" out of optimism, and amber becomes an early, normal signal rather than an embarrassment.

2. Use the same five blocks every week

Project status, week 23                            Status: AMBER

1. Summary         One sentence: where we are and what changed.
2. Forecast        85% date: 14 March (last week: 7 March). Why it moved.
3. Done / next     Three things finished, three things next.
4. Top risks       Max. three, each with owner and next step.
5. Decisions       What we need, from whom, by when.

The fixed order matters. Readers learn where to look, and the most important block, decisions needed, is never buried in paragraph six.

3. Make "decisions needed" impossible to miss

Every open decision gets a name and a date: "Approve the reduced report scope, Head of Finance, by Friday." Projects rarely fail because the team stops working. They fail because a decision waited three weeks in someone's inbox.

A status report is not a diary. It is a request for the help the project needs this week.

4. Show movement, not just position

"85% date: 14 March" tells the position. "Last week: 7 March" tells the direction, and direction is what stakeholders care about. A forecast that moves a week later every week is a red flag, even if the colour is still amber.

5. Keep it to one page

Anything longer goes into a linked appendix: the full risk log, the detailed plan, the backlog. The one page is the conversation starter. If a stakeholder needs more, they will ask, and that is a better meeting than one spent reading slides together.
