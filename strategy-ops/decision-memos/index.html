Decision making

Decision memos: how to stop re-opening the same decisions

Growing companies rarely suffer from a lack of decisions. They suffer from decisions that do not stay made. A one-page memo and a simple log fix most of that.

DataKraft · 5 min read

A familiar pattern: a topic is discussed, everyone nods, the meeting moves on. Three weeks later the same topic is back, because someone was not in the room, remembers it differently, or simply never agreed. The time lost is not the meeting itself. It is the weeks of half-hearted work in between.

The one-page decision memo

For any decision that affects more than one team or costs real money, someone writes one page before the meeting. Not a slide deck; one page that can be read in five minutes.

Decision memo

1. The question        What exactly are we deciding? One sentence.
2. Why now             What happens if we do not decide this week?
3. Options             Two or three real options, including "do nothing".
4. Recommendation      Which option, and the two strongest reasons.
5. Risks               What could go wrong, and how we would notice.
6. Owner and date      Who carries it out, and when we check the result.

Writing it forces clarity. Half of all memos change while being written, because the author discovers the real question is a different one.

Decide in the meeting, record it the same day

Everyone reads the memo at the start of the meeting, in silence, for five minutes. Then the discussion is about the recommendation, not about understanding the problem. The decision, the owner and the review date go into a shared decision log the same day.

The rule that makes it work

A decision in the log can be re-opened at any time, but only with a new fact, not a new opinion.

New facts are welcome: a customer reacted differently than expected, a cost turned out higher, the market moved. "I still think we should have done the other thing" is not a new fact. Saying this rule out loud, once, changes the tone of leadership meetings surprisingly quickly.

Review on the date you set

Each decision has a review date. On that date, look at what happened and keep, change or reverse it, on purpose. Reversing a decision is fine. Drifting away from it without anyone noticing is what costs companies time.

This works best together with a short, structured weekly meeting; see a weekly business review that takes 30 minutes.
