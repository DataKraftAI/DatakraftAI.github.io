Analysis

Start with the decision, not the data

Most analysis time is lost before the first chart: on questions that are too broad, data that answers the wrong thing, and reports nobody asked for. Four questions fix most of it.

DataKraft · 5 min read

A request arrives: "Can you look into why sales are down?" It sounds clear. It is not. Down compared to what? Which sales? And what will anyone do with the answer? Without that, an analyst can spend a week producing thirty charts that are all correct and none of which change anything.

Before I open any data, I ask four questions.

1. Which decision will this inform?

Every useful analysis supports a decision: spend more or less, change a price, stop a product, hire or not. If nobody can name the decision, the analysis is curiosity. That can be fine, but it should be quick and labelled as such.

2. Who makes that decision?

The answer shapes everything: the level of detail, the language, the format. A head of sales needs a different page than a pricing analyst, even from the same data.

3. By when?

A good answer on Thursday beats a perfect answer after the budget meeting. Knowing the deadline tells you how deep to go and which data you can realistically use.

4. What result would change their mind?

This is the most powerful question. Ask the decision maker: "What would you need to see to decide differently?" The answer tells you exactly which number matters, and often reveals that most of the planned analysis is not needed.

Example: "If marketplace orders are less profitable than own-shop orders after returns and fees, I would move budget away from the marketplace." Now the analysis is one comparison, done carefully, instead of a general review of all sales.

Write it down in five lines

Decision      Shift Q3 promotion budget between channels?
Decided by    Head of E-commerce
Needed by     Thursday, before the budget meeting
Would change  Margin per order by channel, after returns and fees
Out of scope  Product-level pricing (separate analysis)

Send it to the requester and ask "is this right?" before starting. It takes five minutes and saves days.

The best analysts are not the ones with the most charts. They are the ones whose charts change decisions.

Then: check the data before trusting it

Once the question is clear, the next risk is the data itself: duplicates, gaps and definitions that differ between systems. The data health check demo shows the kind of checks I run first.
