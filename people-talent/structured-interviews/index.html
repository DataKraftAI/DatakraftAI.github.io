Interviewing

Structured interviews in practice: fairer decisions in less time

Unstructured interviews feel natural, but they mostly measure how much the interviewer liked the conversation. A little structure makes hiring both fairer and faster.

DataKraft · 5 min read

Research on hiring has shown for decades that structured interviews predict job performance better than free-flowing conversations. Yet many teams still improvise: each interviewer asks what comes to mind, and the debrief becomes a discussion of impressions. Here is how I set up structure without making interviews feel like an exam.

1. Split the scorecard across the interviewers

Start from the role scorecard (the three to five outcomes for year one). Give each interviewer one or two outcomes to cover. Nobody covers everything, and no question is asked twice.

2. Same questions for every candidate

For each outcome, write two or three questions that ask for real past examples. Every candidate for the role gets the same questions, in roughly the same order. Follow-up questions are fine; changing the main questions is not.

Outcome: bring renewal rate for mid-size customers to 90%

Q1  Tell me about a time you improved customer retention.
    What did you measure, what did you change, what happened?
Q2  How did you find out which customers were at risk?
Q3  Describe something you tried that did not work. What did you learn?

3. A rating guide written in advance

For each question, describe what a weak, solid and strong answer looks like. It takes half an hour to write and removes most of the "I just had a good feeling" from the debrief.

ScoreWhat it looks like (example for Q1)
1 · weakGeneral statements, no example, no numbers
3 · solidA real example with a clear action and a result
5 · strongSeveral examples, measured results, can explain why it worked and what they would do differently

4. Score alone, then discuss

Every interviewer submits their scores and notes before the debrief. Otherwise the most senior or the most confident voice sets the tone, and everyone else adjusts. In the debrief, look first at the outcomes where scores differ most. That is where the real discussion is.

The goal of a debrief is not to agree quickly. It is to find out where we disagree, and why.

5. Keep the candidate experience human

  • Tell candidates in advance what each interview covers. Good candidates prepare better, nobody is surprised.
  • Leave ten minutes for the candidate's own questions in every round.
  • Send a decision within 48 hours of the last interview, including a short reason if it is a no.

Structured interviews take a little more preparation once, and then save time on every hire: shorter debriefs, fewer extra rounds "just to be sure", and decisions you can explain.
