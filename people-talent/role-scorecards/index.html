Hiring

Write the scorecard before the job ad: hiring for outcomes, not wish lists

Most hiring problems start before the first applicant arrives: with a job ad that lists twelve requirements and no clear idea of what success looks like.

DataKraft · 6 min read

When a role opens, the fastest route is to copy the last job ad, add a few skills and publish. The result is familiar: too many applicants who do not fit, too few who do, and interviewers who each look for something different. A role scorecard fixes this, and it takes about an hour with the hiring manager.

What a scorecard is

A scorecard describes what the person must achieve in their first year, not what they must have. Three to five outcomes, each specific enough that you could tell after twelve months whether it happened.

Wish list (typical job ad)Outcome (scorecard)
5+ years of customer success experienceBring renewal rate for mid-size customers from 82% to 90% within 12 months
Excellent communication skillsRun quarterly business reviews with the 20 largest accounts, with written follow-ups
Experience with CRM toolsSet up health scores in the CRM so at-risk accounts are visible 90 days before renewal

Add the evidence for each outcome

For every outcome, write down what would convince you that a candidate can deliver it. This becomes the backbone of the interviews.

Outcome: bring renewal rate from 82% to 90%.
Evidence: has improved retention before and can explain how, with numbers; can describe how they found the accounts at risk; can name something that did not work and why.

Then the job ad writes itself

With the scorecard in hand, the job ad becomes concrete: "In your first year you will…" followed by the outcomes. Candidates can judge for themselves whether they fit. You will usually get fewer, but much better, applications, and more people from unexpected backgrounds who can do the job but would not have ticked every box on a wish list.

A wish list filters for people who look like the last hire. A scorecard filters for people who can do the next job.

Use it in every interview and in the decision

  • Each interviewer covers specific outcomes, so nobody asks the same question twice.
  • Everyone scores against the same outcomes, so the debrief compares evidence, not impressions.
  • The offer conversation can refer to the outcomes, so expectations are clear from day one.

And when the new hire starts, the scorecard turns into their first-year goals almost word for word.
